# Chapter 6 - Discussion and Impact

Chapter 5 established what this project's calibrated ray-tracing pipeline achieves and why: which sites and frequencies it predicts well, which mechanisms (scattering, terrain resolution, calibration-landscape difficulty) govern that accuracy, and how the results compare against classical models, standards, and the literature this project's methodology directly builds on. This chapter does not repeat that analysis. It asks what those specific results mean: what they add to the scientific understanding of calibrated ray tracing, how they could inform engineering practice, and what broader implications follow, cautiously, from a project whose direct measurements were technical rather than economic or environmental. Every claim in this chapter is tied to a specific result already established in Chapters 3 to 5, not to the ray-tracing field in general.

## 6.1 Scientific Contributions

**A quantified boundary condition for when material calibration matters.** The most useful scientific output of this project is not any single R² value but the boundary it draws, empirically, between sites where material calibration is necessary and sites where it is not. At Nottingham and London, joint material optimisation (Powell or CMA-ES) was required to reach an acceptable result; at Stevenage, across all four measured frequencies, no material search improved on ITU-R P.2040-2 [17] defaults, and the CMA-ES joint search was independently confirmed to converge to "no change" rather than simply not being tried (Section 5.2). This project's methodology is built on the staged scalar-offset-then-material-refinement architecture reported by Hoydis et al. [3], [21]; the contribution here is to show that the second stage, joint material refinement, is conditional rather than always necessary, and specifically conditional on whether a site is diffuse-scattering-dominated or specular-dominated. Stevenage's coherent-summation result at all four of its measured frequencies, the only site in this project where that holds, is the direct evidence for that condition.

**A corrected, project-specific explanation for ray tracing's advantage over a classical model.** Section 5.3 reported that calibrated ray tracing outperforms COST 231 Walfisch-Ikegami at Nottingham 915 MHz not because the site falls outside the classical model's stated validity range, but because Walfisch-Ikegami's uniform-building-row assumption is violated by Nottingham's mixed-height urban core. This correction matters specifically because an earlier working assumption in this project's own notes had attributed the discrepancy to a frequency-range violation, a claim this thesis checked directly against COST 231's documented 800-2000 MHz envelope [10] and found to be false (`references.md`, "Not used" section). The controlled, like-for-like comparison behind this correction used the same scene and the same measurements throughout, so the result is specific to this project's own data, not a general claim about ray tracing versus classical models.

**A frequency-dependent limit on geometric vegetation modelling, established across this project's own scenes.** The requirement to disable solid canopy geometry at 3602 MHz (`DISABLE_CANOPY=True`, Section 3.3), replacing it with a per-path ITU-R P.833-10 [16] correction, was not a modelling convenience but a response to a physical limit this project measured directly: at λ = 8.3 cm, solid canopy cones were found to absorb essentially all rays beyond roughly 400 m. This was confirmed independently at both Stevenage and Nottingham's dedicated high-frequency scene, giving a concrete, wavelength-anchored point, drawn from this project's own results, at which a 3D geometric vegetation model stops being the right abstraction and a per-path statistical correction becomes necessary instead.

**Cross-validation between this project's own two implementations.** Nottingham 915 MHz was evaluated by two pipelines built within this project at different times, using different ray-tracing engine versions and different calibration methods (Section 5.3): a Sionna 0.19.2 differentiable, scalar-offset-only pipeline (5.72 dB RMSE) and the project's main Sionna 2.0 DEM pipeline with Powell material calibration (6.0 dB RMSE). Neither pipeline was tuned to match the other, so their close agreement is itself evidence that this project's calibration approach is robust to implementation choices, not only a restatement of one pipeline's accuracy.

## 6.2 Engineering and Practical Implications

**Relevance to the problem this thesis set out to address.** Chapter 1 identified dense-urban, small-cell-scale wireless planning as poorly served by coarse, statistical models [9]-[11]. This project's best result, an RMSE of 6.0 dB, below the 3GPP TR 38.901 shadow-fading floor of 7.82 dB [6] (Section 5.3), shows that at a well-characterised site, this calibrated ray-tracing methodology can predict path loss more precisely than the uncertainty a purely statistical model would already assume for that same environment.

**An actionable finding from this project's own scattering-sensitivity results.** Section 5.2 found that disabling diffuse scattering is not a minor simplification but, at dense urban NLOS sites, catastrophic: R² collapsed to -22.666 at London 915 MHz once scattering was switched off (Table 5.2). Any use of this project's methodology, or one built on the same modelling assumptions, at a similarly dense NLOS site should expect a comparably large failure if diffuse scattering is omitted, not a small accuracy loss. The Stevenage result offers the complementary caution: at that specular-dominated site, material calibration bought no measurable improvement, so this project's own data argues for matching modelling effort to the site's propagation regime rather than defaulting to the most complex configuration everywhere.

**Constraints this project's own results place on applying this methodology elsewhere.** Three limitations identified in Section 5.4 bound how directly these results transfer to a new site. First, terrain data quality is a hard constraint rather than a tunable parameter: Scar Hill's order-of-magnitude lower R² reflects its 30 m SRTM terrain input against the 1 m EA LiDAR available at the English sites [8], [30], so this methodology's achievable accuracy at a new site depends on the terrain data actually available there. Second, calibration in this project was not a one-shot procedure: London 1802 MHz required eight independent CMA-ES runs before a physically defensible result was accepted (Table 5.3), and comparable multi-attempt calibration histories are documented at other sites in this project's own working notes. Third, the computational cost this project incurred, tens of millions of ray samples per evaluation, GPU-resident, is a real cost that would need to be weighed against the scale of any application of this same methodology.

## 6.3 Environmental, Economic, or Societal Impacts

This project measured technical accuracy only. It did not measure cost, energy use, or social outcomes, so the observations below are limited to what this project's own results specifically support, rather than a general assessment of ray tracing's environmental, economic, or societal role.

**Environmental.** This project's own evaluations used measurement data already collected by an existing campaign, the Ofcom 2018 dataset [5], rather than new drive-test data collected for this thesis. Beyond noting that a calibrated model of an already-measured site does not, by itself, require re-measuring that site, this project has no basis to estimate a broader environmental effect, and none is claimed.

**Economic.** The calibration-necessity finding in Section 6.1 has a direct, narrow economic reading: for a site resembling Stevenage, this project's own results show that the added effort of material-parameter calibration produced no measurable accuracy gain, whereas for sites resembling Nottingham or London, that same effort was necessary to reach an acceptable result. This project's own data therefore supports checking a site's propagation regime, specular- versus diffuse-dominated, before committing to a full calibration effort, rather than a general claim about the economics of ray-tracing adoption.

**Societal.** The one impact claim this project can make directly, rather than by extension, is the terrain-data-quality gap documented in Section 5.4: Scar Hill's rural, SRTM-covered site achieved an R² an order of magnitude below the LiDAR-covered English sites [30]. This is this project's own comparative result, not a general claim about rural connectivity: it shows that, at least for the specific methodology and sites evaluated here, the accuracy this approach can deliver is not uniform across urban and rural terrain-data coverage, and any application of this methodology to a new rural site should expect the same terrain-driven limitation observed at Scar Hill.
