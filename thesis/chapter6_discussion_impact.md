# Chapter 6 - Discussion and Impact

Chapter 5 established what this project's calibrated ray-tracing pipeline achieves and why: which sites and frequencies it predicts well, which mechanisms (scattering, terrain resolution, calibration-landscape difficulty) govern that accuracy, and how the results hold up against classical models, standards, and independent literature. This chapter does not repeat that analysis. Instead, it asks a different question: what does any of it mean, beyond the numbers in Table 5.1? Section 6.1 considers the scientific knowledge this project adds to the ray-tracing and radio-propagation literature. Section 6.2 considers how the results could inform engineering practice, and under what conditions. Section 6.3 considers the environmental, economic, and societal implications that follow, cautiously, from a project whose direct measurements were technical rather than economic or environmental. Throughout, the aim is to state plainly what this thesis's findings support and, just as importantly, what they do not yet support.

## 6.1 Scientific Contributions

**A quantified boundary condition for when material calibration matters.** Perhaps the most useful scientific output of this project is not any single R² value but the boundary it draws between sites where calibration is necessary and sites where it is not. At Nottingham and London, joint material optimisation (Powell or CMA-ES) was required to reach an acceptable result; at Stevenage, across all four measured frequencies, no material search improved on ITU-R P.2040-2 [17] defaults, and the CMA-ES joint search was independently confirmed to converge to "no change" rather than simply not being tried (Section 5.2). This is a genuine, falsifiable finding rather than an assumption carried over from the calibration literature: Hoydis et al.'s staged scalar-offset-then-material architecture [3], [21] is shown here to generalise across five independently measured environments, but its second stage, joint material refinement, is shown to be conditional on the propagation regime, specifically on whether the site is diffuse-scattering-dominated or specular-dominated. Stevenage's coherent-summation result at all four of its frequencies (the only site in this project where this holds) is the clearest evidence for that condition, and it is a result this project measured directly rather than assumed from prior work.

**Evidence on where ray tracing's advantage over classical models actually comes from.** Section 5.3 already reported that calibrated ray tracing outperforms COST 231 Walfisch-Ikegami at Nottingham 915 MHz not because the site falls outside the classical model's stated validity range, but because Walfisch-Ikegami's uniform-building-row assumption is violated by Nottingham's mixed-height urban core. The contribution here is broader than that single comparison: an earlier working assumption in this project's own notes attributed the discrepancy to a frequency-range violation, a claim that this thesis traced, checked against COST 231's documented 800-2000 MHz envelope [10], and found to be false (`references.md`, "Not used" section). Correcting that assumption, rather than repeating it, matters because the same error (attributing ray tracing's advantage to classical models being "misapplied" rather than to geometry they cannot represent) appears to recur informally in adjacent literature and project notes. This thesis's contribution is to show, with a controlled like-for-like comparison using the same scene and the same measurements, that the real explanation is architectural, not spectral.

**A frequency-dependent limit on geometric vegetation modelling.** The requirement to disable solid canopy geometry at 3602 MHz (`DISABLE_CANOPY=True`, Section 3.3), replacing it with a per-path ITU-R P.833-10 [16] correction, was not a modelling convenience but a response to a genuine physical limit: at λ = 8.3 cm, tree-branch structures approach the ray tracer's geometric resolution, and solid canopy cones were found to absorb essentially all rays beyond roughly 400 m. Because this was independently confirmed at both Stevenage and Nottingham's high-frequency scene, this project offers a concrete, wavelength-anchored answer to a question the ITU-R vegetation-attenuation recommendation [16] does not itself address: at what point does a 3D geometric vegetation model stop being the right abstraction, and a per-path statistical correction become necessary instead.

**Independent cross-validation between two implementations.** Nottingham 915 MHz was evaluated by two implementations built at different times, using different ray-tracing engine versions and different calibration methods (Section 5.3): a Sionna 0.19.2 differentiable, scalar-offset-only pipeline (5.72 dB RMSE) and the project's main Sionna 2.0 DEM pipeline with Powell material calibration (6.0 dB RMSE). Neither implementation was tuned to match the other, so their agreement is itself evidence, not merely a restatement of one pipeline's accuracy. Reporting this kind of cross-pipeline agreement is comparatively rare in single-implementation ray-tracing theses, including the closest directly comparable prior work, a CPU-only, Blender-based Sionna RT study of Bologna [26], which validates one pipeline against one city without an independent second implementation.

**Scale and diversity of the validation base.** This project's five sites (Nottingham, London, Scar Hill, Stevenage, Southampton) and fourteen site/frequency combinations, all against independently collected, public Ofcom measurements [5], represent a validation base broader than is typical for a ray-tracing thesis of this kind. This scale is what makes the cross-site finding in Section 5.2 credible: that achievable accuracy is explained by identifiable physical differences (building density, terrain data quality, specular-versus-diffuse balance) rather than by an arbitrary difference in calibration effort. A single-site study could not have distinguished those two explanations from each other.

## 6.2 Engineering and Practical Implications

**Where this methodology is directly relevant today.** The clearest near-term application is small-cell and dense-urban 5G/6G network planning, the same use case Chapter 1 identified as poorly served by coarse, statistical models [9]-[11]. This project's best result (RMSE = 6.0 dB, below the 3GPP TR 38.901 shadow-fading floor of 7.82 dB [6], Section 5.3) shows that calibrated ray tracing can, at a well-characterised site, predict path loss more precisely than the uncertainty a purely statistical channel model would already assume. A second, increasingly relevant application is digital twin network platforms [4], where a live, differentiable propagation model of a real environment supports what-if planning (new base stations, changed building geometry) without new physical measurement campaigns for every scenario. A third is vegetation-aware planning for suburban and rural links, where this project's frequency-dependent vegetation treatment (Section 3.3) offers a concrete recipe rather than a single blanket attenuation figure.

**What these results change in practice, and what they do not.** The scattering-sensitivity finding of Section 5.2 is directly actionable for anyone using or building a ray-tracing planning tool: at dense urban NLOS sites, a specular-only model is not a simplified approximation but, based on this project's own measurements, catastrophically wrong (R² collapsing to -22.666 at London 915 MHz without scattering, Table 5.2). Any planning tool that omits diffuse scattering at such sites should be expected to fail in a similarly dramatic way, not merely lose some accuracy. Conversely, the Stevenage result cautions against always reaching for the most complex available model: at a specular-dominated suburban site, the added cost of material calibration bought no measurable improvement, so matching the modelling effort to the site's propagation regime, rather than defaulting to maximum complexity everywhere, is itself a practical decision this project's data can inform.

**Constraints that bound real deployment.** Three limitations identified in Section 5.4 carry directly into practice. First, terrain data quality is a hard constraint on achievable accuracy, not a tunable parameter: Scar Hill's order-of-magnitude lower R² reflects its 30 m SRTM terrain input against the 1 m EA LiDAR available at English sites [8], [30], meaning this methodology's practical accuracy depends on terrain data availability that varies substantially by country and by how remote a site is. Second, calibration is not a one-shot procedure. London 1802 MHz required eight independent CMA-ES runs before a physically defensible result was accepted (Table 5.3), and this project's own working history documents comparable multi-attempt calibration efforts at other sites; an operational deployment of this methodology needs to budget for that iteration, not assume the first run succeeds. Third, the computational cost (tens of millions of ray samples per evaluation, GPU-resident) needs to be weighed against the scale of the deployment it supports; this methodology is well suited to characterising a site archetype once and reusing that calibration, less so to real-time, per-request coverage prediction at that same computational cost.

**A plausible adoption pathway.** Rather than a single calibration run per new site, a more scalable path suggested by this project's own results is to calibrate representative material libraries per environment archetype (dense mixed-height urban, suburban new-town, rural hilltop) and reuse them as informed starting priors for new sites of a similar type, reducing the number of calibration attempts a new deployment needs. Longer term, the three-phase calibration procedure documented in Section 3.4 (scalar offset, joint material search, re-scalar) is a candidate building block for a standardised RT-based planning workflow that could sit alongside, rather than replace, the classical models [9]-[11] still embedded in current planning tools and guidance.

## 6.3 Environmental, Economic, or Societal Impacts

This project measured technical accuracy, not cost, emissions, or social outcomes, so the claims in this section are stated as plausible, reasoned implications of the technical findings, not as measured results in their own right.

**Environmental.** Every site in this project's validation base was originally characterised by physical drive-test measurement campaigns, including the Ofcom dataset [5] this thesis reuses. A validated, calibrated ray-tracing model of a given environment archetype reduces, in principle, how often that kind of vehicle-based survey needs to be repeated for future planning decisions at similar sites, since a well-characterised material library can be reapplied rather than re-measured from scratch. Separately, more accurate coverage prediction supports better-targeted small-cell placement instead of blanket over-provisioning; if fewer, better-placed base stations can deliver the same coverage, the plausible downstream effect is lower aggregate network energy consumption, though this project did not measure energy use and this connection should be read as a hypothesis for future work, not a demonstrated saving.

**Economic.** The clearest economic implication follows directly from the calibration-effort finding in Section 6.1: at sites like Stevenage, where ITU-R defaults already suffice, operators can skip costly material-parameter optimisation entirely, while at sites like Nottingham and London, the calibration effort is justified by a measurable accuracy gain. Knowing in advance which category a new site is likely to fall into, for instance from its building-density and terrain-data profile, is itself a resource-allocation aid. More broadly, this project's reliance on open data and open-source tooling (Ofcom's public measurement archive [5], UK Environment Agency LiDAR [8], OpenStreetMap geometry, and the open-source Sionna RT framework [3]) lowers the barrier to entry for organisations that cannot afford proprietary commercial planning software or bespoke measurement campaigns, which is a meaningful economic-accessibility consideration for smaller operators, municipalities, or academic groups.

**Societal.** The terrain-data-quality gap documented in Section 5.4, Scar Hill's SRTM-limited accuracy against the LiDAR-supported English sites [30], is not only a technical limitation but a finding with an equity dimension: rural and remote areas that lack high-resolution terrain data will, by this project's own evidence, receive systematically less accurate ray-tracing-based planning than urban areas that already have LiDAR coverage. Left unaddressed, that gap risks compounding an existing rural connectivity disadvantage rather than helping to close it, an observation grounded directly in this project's own comparative result rather than in general concern about the digital divide. Separately, accurate coverage prediction in dense urban NLOS pockets, precisely where this project found classical statistical models least reliable (Section 5.3), is relevant to public-safety and emergency-communications planning, where undetected coverage gaps carry a safety cost beyond the commercial one. Finally, building this methodology on public datasets and open-source software, rather than proprietary black-box tools, supports a more transparent and independently reproducible basis for infrastructure and spectrum-policy decisions, a property this thesis's own reproducibility effort (Chapter 3, Chapter 4) was built around from the outset.
