# Chapter 7 - Conclusion and Future Work

This chapter closes the thesis. Section 7.1 restates, briefly, what this project found, without re-deriving the analysis already given in Chapters 5 and 6. Section 7.2 returns to the four research questions and the seven objectives set out in Section 1.5 and states plainly, for each one, whether this project's own results answer it fully, partially, or not at all; a strong conclusion says this honestly rather than presenting every objective as fully met. Section 7.3 identifies the limitations this project's own evidence points to and proposes concrete, achievable next steps, not a general survey of where ray tracing could go.

## 7.1 Summary of Research Findings

This project built and calibrated a ray-tracing propagation pipeline using Sionna RT [3], evaluated it against real, independently collected drive-test measurements from the Ofcom 2018 campaign [5] at five UK sites across up to four frequencies each (915-3602 MHz), and validated it against a classical empirical model [10] and a 3GPP standard [6].

The headline result is not a single accuracy figure but a characterised range: R² from 0.083 (Scar Hill, terrain-limited [30]) to 0.835 (Nottingham 915 MHz, RMSE 6.0 dB, below the 3GPP shadow-fading floor of 7.82 dB [6]), with the variation between sites and frequencies traced to identifiable physical causes rather than left unexplained (Section 5.2, Section 6.1). Three findings from this project stand out as genuinely new rather than expected: first, that material calibration measurably helps at some sites (Nottingham, London, Southampton) and provably does not at others (Stevenage, at all four of its frequencies), a boundary tied to whether the site is diffuse-scattering-dominated or specular-dominated; second, that ray tracing's advantage over COST 231 Walfisch-Ikegami [10] at Nottingham comes from representing building-height irregularity, not from the site falling outside COST 231's stated validity range, a correction of an assumption this project's own earlier notes had made; third, that geometric vegetation modelling stops being a viable abstraction at 3602 MHz (λ = 8.3 cm), requiring a per-path ITU-R P.833-10 [16] correction instead of solid canopy geometry.

## 7.2 Conclusions Related to Research Questions

**RQ1: How accurately can Sionna RT generate high-resolution radio environmental maps within complex UK urban environments?** Fully addressed. Accuracy was quantified at every one of the five sites and fourteen site/frequency combinations evaluated (Table 5.1), with the best result (R² = 0.835) exceeding the 3GPP shadow-fading floor and the worst (R² = 0.083) attributable to a specific, identified cause (terrain data resolution) rather than an unexplained limit of the method.

**RQ2: How do architectural features affect signal propagation?** Fully addressed. Nottingham's non-monotonic accuracy across frequency was tied directly to changes in vegetation density and canopy geometry (Section 5.2); the corrected explanation for ray tracing's advantage over COST 231 (Section 6.1) shows building-height irregularity, specifically, as the architectural feature classical models cannot represent; and the cross-site comparison (Section 5.2) ties Nottingham's and London's dense, mixed-height cores, Stevenage's more uniform new-town geometry, and Scar Hill's rural terrain to their respective results.

**RQ3: What spatial variations in signal strength, path loss, delay spread, and multipath richness can be observed across different urban regions?** Partially addressed. Spatial variation in path loss and signal strength across distance and across sites was fully characterised (Tables 5.1-5.2, Section 5.2). Multipath richness was characterised indirectly, through the scattering ON/OFF sensitivity comparison (Table 5.2) and the scatter-path-count ratios documented during calibration (Section 4.4, Table 5.3), rather than through a dedicated multipath-richness metric. Delay spread and angular dispersion, both named in this project's own objectives (Section 1.5), were not computed or reported in this implementation; Sionna's `PathSolver` output supports this in principle, but it was not exercised for that purpose here. This is stated plainly as an objective not met, not implied to have been achieved.

**RQ4: How do RT-based predictions compare with classical models in accuracy, resolution, and applicability?** Addressed for the one site where a controlled, like-for-like comparison was carried out. At Nottingham 915 MHz, calibrated ray tracing (5.72 dB RMSE, an independent differentiable-RT pipeline) outperformed COST 231 Walfisch-Ikegami (approximately 12-18 dB RMSE) for a specific, identified reason, building-height irregularity, using the same scene and the same measurements (Section 5.3, Section 6.1). This comparison was not repeated at the other four sites or at higher frequencies, so this thesis's own evidence supports the conclusion at Nottingham 915 MHz specifically, not a general claim that ray tracing outperforms classical models everywhere this project was evaluated.

**Objectives.** Of the seven objectives set out in Section 1.5: developing three-dimensional urban models from LiDAR and OpenStreetMap data, preparing scenes for Sionna RT, conducting the propagation simulations themselves, evaluating the applicability and limitations of the framework, and investigating how calibration and geometric refinement affect RMSE and R² were each fully achieved, and are the substance of Chapters 4 to 6. Generating radio environmental maps was achieved at the level of methodology, using Sionna's `RadioMapSolver` (Section 3.4, Step 6), but the rendered coverage-map figures themselves remain outstanding in this written thesis (Figures 3.7, 4.4), pending generation from the notebooks directly. Analysing path loss, multipath propagation, delay spread, and angular dispersion was only partially achieved, for the reason stated under RQ3 above: path loss and a multipath-sensitivity proxy were delivered, delay spread and angular dispersion were not.

## 7.3 Recommendations and Future Research Directions

**Limitations this project's own evidence identifies.** Three bound how far these results generalise, each already established with evidence in Chapters 5 and 6 and restated here only as the basis for what follows: achievable accuracy depends on terrain data resolution, which varies categorically between sites (1 m LiDAR versus 30 m SRTM [30]); calibration is not a one-shot procedure, with London 1802 MHz requiring eight CMA-ES [31] runs before an acceptable result was accepted; and the computational cost of tens of millions of ray samples per evaluation bounds how many sites or configurations can practically be explored in a given study.

**Recommendations for improving the methodology.** The calibration-necessity boundary found at Stevenage (Section 6.1) suggests that future work should screen a new site's propagation regime, specular- versus diffuse-dominated, before committing to a full material-parameter search, rather than applying the same calibration effort everywhere by default. Where a full search is warranted, reusing a calibrated material library from a previously characterised site of similar type (dense urban, suburban new-town, rural hilltop) as a warm start is a concrete way to reduce the number of calibration attempts needed, rather than starting from ITU-R defaults each time.

**Concrete next steps for this project's own dataset.** Two evaluations begun during this project were not completed at the time of writing and are the most direct next step: Southampton's 2695 and 3602 MHz notebooks, and London's 2695 MHz calibration. Completing them would extend the fourteen-combination result set reported here to full coverage of all five sites at all four frequencies.

**Concrete future research directions.** First, the RQ3 gap identified in Section 7.2, delay spread and angular dispersion, is directly addressable using the same `PathSolver` per-path output already used in this project's per-path vegetation correction (Section 3.4), and would let a future study characterise multipath richness quantitatively rather than through the scattering-sensitivity proxy used here. Second, the RQ4 classical-model comparison was carried out at a single site and frequency; repeating it at the other four sites, and above 3602 MHz, would test whether the building-height-irregularity explanation for ray tracing's advantage holds generally or is specific to Nottingham's geometry. Third, testing whether the Stevenage-like "no calibration needed" finding recurs at other specular-dominated suburban sites, beyond the single site where it was observed here, would establish whether it is a property of that environment type or particular to Stevenage itself.
